## Fields (the minimum)

| Section | Fields |
|---|---|
| Contact | Email |
| Shipping | Address (autocomplete), full name, phone (optional) |
| Payment | Card number, expiry, CVC |

- **Billing address:** it defaults to the shipping address, with no checkbox. Card details alone are usually enough. Only ask for a billing ZIP if your processor needs it for AVS.
- **Account creation:** none at checkout. After payment, offer "Save your details? Set a password" on the confirmation page.
- **Shipping method:** one default method, preselected and not shown as a choice. If you offer several speeds, add a single compact row under the address instead of a separate step.

## Behavior that does the real work

1. **Express pay at the top.** Apple Pay, Google Pay and PayPal collect email, address and payment in one tap, so many customers never see the form.
2. **Single column, one screen on mobile.** No stepper or progress bar, since there's only one step.
3. **Address autocomplete.** One field expands into the full address only when needed.
4. **Inline validation on blur.** Show errors beside the field, never in a banner. Keep the user's input on error.
5. **Correct input types.** Use `type=email`, `inputmode=numeric` and `autocomplete` attributes (`cc-number`, `shipping postal-code`, etc.) so browsers and password managers fill the form.
6. **Card field.** Auto-format it, detect the brand, and advance focus from number to expiry to CVC.
7. **Sticky pay button on mobile.** The button label is the total ("Pay $84.50"), which replaces the order summary and shipping line. The total is the only price shown, and it includes shipping and tax.
8. **Loading and success.** The button shows a spinner on submit and is disabled to prevent double charges. On success, go straight to a confirmation page with the order number and an emailed receipt.
9. **Guest-first.** Returning users get autofill via saved cards or Shop Pay and skip the typing.

## Removed, as requested

## Things to check before shipping

These removals can create legal or support problems depending on where you sell:

- **Total price transparency.** Showing only the total is fine, but the total must clearly include shipping and tax. In the EU, UK and some US states, the final price must be shown before purchase. Hiding the shipping line can also raise "where did this number come from?" support tickets.
- **Terms acceptance.** Many jurisdictions accept implied consent. A small line of text under the button replaces the checkbox without adding friction: *"By paying, you agree to our Terms."* This is not a checkbox. Subscriptions or recurring charges usually need explicit consent, so check with legal.
- **Return policy.** Put it in the footer, the confirmation email or the product page. EU distance-selling rules require withdrawal-right information to be provided, though not necessarily on this page.
- **Cart review.** With no order summary, users can't catch a wrong quantity at the last moment. Consider a collapsed "1 item ▾" toggle, or make sure the cart page is clear. I'd measure the refund and cancellation rate after launch.

## How to validate

A/B test the new page against the current flow. Track checkout completion rate, time to complete, payment error rate, and post-purchase support contacts and refunds. The last two will show whether removing the summary and shipping line cost you anything.

## Risks to check before shipping
Several of the removals can cause problems. Check them first:

1. **Total only, no shipping line.** Showing one all-in total is fine in most places. Some jurisdictions, and card-network or payment-provider rules, expect that shipping and tax are not hidden or revealed late. The total on the button must be the final charge, with no surprise additions afterward.
2. **No order summary.** Customers can't confirm what they're buying, so you may see more "wrong item or quantity" refunds and chargebacks. A cheap fix is a one-line collapsed item count and thumbnail above the form, such as "2 items ▸".
3. **No terms checkbox.** Many sites use "browsewrap" wording under the button instead, such as "By paying you agree to our Terms". That keeps the page minimal, and it's often enough, but check with your legal team. Some regions (the EU, for example) require explicit consent for certain things, such as digital goods and the right of withdrawal.
4. **No return-policy link.** Return-policy visibility near the point of purchase reduces hesitation and also helps with some consumer-protection rules. A small footer link costs almost nothing in clutter.
